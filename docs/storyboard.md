# PM2.5 Europe Visual Storyboard

## Core Story

Europe's PM2.5 pollution has improved considerably since 2007, but the improvement is uneven and important regional hotspots remain.

The reader journey should move from personal relevance to continental change, then from the continental pattern to the regions where progress is incomplete. The structure should answer one question at a time:

1. Why should I care about PM2.5?
2. Where does it come from?
3. Was Europe more polluted in 2007?
4. Has Europe improved since then?
5. If Europe improved, why are hotspots still visible?
6. Which regions remain important?
7. What can I explore myself?

The final piece should feel like a guided visual investigation, not a dashboard. The reader should never see all maps at once before understanding why they matter.

# WOW Moments

These moments should make the story feel memorable, guided and worth scrolling through. They are not decorative tricks; each one should reveal something important.

## 1. The Opening Map Under the Headline

The first screen should feel like entering a large European air-quality map before the reader knows exactly what it means. The headline supplies the contradiction: cleaner, but not equally. This creates immediate tension.

## 2. PM2.5 Becomes Visible

Tiny particles are difficult to imagine. A simple animated particle-size comparison can make the invisible visible, moving from a large everyday reference to PM2.5. This turns an abstract pollutant into something bodily and personal.

## 3. Source Cards Build the World Behind the Map

As the reader scrolls, heating, transport, industry and agriculture appear one by one. The moment should feel like the map is being prepared: before seeing hotspots, readers understand the systems that can create them.

## 4. First Reveal of the 2007 Baseline

The first actual PM2.5 map should arrive slowly, with the 2007 label appearing before the annotations. This is the first data reveal and should feel like the story has moved from explanation into evidence.

## 5. Animated Europe Transformation

The central WOW moment is the 2007 to 2024 cross-fade. As the reader scrubs or presses Play, Europe visibly lightens. This should be smooth and cinematic, with the year label large enough to feel like a temporal jump.

## 6. Trend Line Confirms the Transformation

After the map transformation, the time-series line draws itself from 2007 to 2024. This is quieter than the map animation, but satisfying: the visual impression becomes evidence.

## 7. Change Map Complicates the Good News

The story should briefly let the reader feel the good news before revealing the uneven change map. This pivot is important: it prevents the narrative from becoming a simple success story.

## 8. Hotspot Reveal

The 2024 map should desaturate or recede, then hotspot areas above 10 ug/m3 should appear. This should feel like the remaining problem is being uncovered from within the cleaner map.

## 9. Guided Regional Zooms

The map should fly from Europe into selected hotspot regions. Each zoom should feel like a camera move in a documentary: overview first, then closer context, then a short annotation.

## 10. Persistence Layer Adds Memory

The persistence map should feel like the story is adding time back into the hotspots. Current hotspots become historical patterns, not isolated marks.

## 11. The Story Hands Control to the Reader

The transition into "Explore yourself" should be clear. The interface opens up, layer toggles appear and the reader can inspect the evidence. This is the Martini Glass shift from guided narrative to exploration.

## 12. Method Note as Trust Moment

The closing methods panel should feel calm and transparent. It is not a legal disclaimer; it is the final act of credibility.

# Visual Language

## Typography

- Use a strong editorial serif for headlines to give the story a visual journalism tone.
- Use a clean sans-serif for annotations, controls, legends, captions and interface labels.
- Keep body text generous and readable, with short paragraphs and clear hierarchy.
- Use large year labels for temporal moments, especially 2007 and 2024.

## Color Palette

- Base palette: off-white, cool grey-blue and deep ink for a calm newsroom feel.
- Data palette: use the existing PM2.5 heat scale consistently so readers learn one visual language for pollution intensity.
- Accent colors:
  - blue/teal for explanation and orientation,
  - orange/red for hotspots and threshold exceedance,
  - purple only where persistence or a secondary analytical layer needs distinction.
- Avoid a one-color theme. The palette should feel analytical, not decorative.

## Icon Style

- Simple line or filled icons with consistent stroke weight.
- Icons should explain concepts, not decorate sections.
- Use icons for PM2.5 size, lungs/health, heating, transport, industry and agriculture.
- Keep icons flat, restrained and publication-like.

## Animation Style

- Motion should be purposeful and calm.
- Use fades, cross-fades, line draws and map fly-to movements.
- Avoid bouncy or playful easing.
- Animation should reveal relationships: time passing, layers appearing, focus narrowing.
- Default duration should be short enough to keep momentum but long enough to feel readable.

## Spacing

- Use generous vertical spacing between major sections to create a cinematic scroll rhythm.
- Keep explanatory text narrow enough for comfortable reading.
- Let maps breathe with large frames or full-width moments.
- Avoid dashboard density in guided sections; save dense controls for the final exploration section.

## Overall Design Inspiration

- Inspired by modern visual journalism from teams such as The New York Times Graphics, Reuters Graphics, Financial Times Visual and Data Journalism, and The Pudding.
- The design should not copy any one publication. It should borrow the principles: clear hierarchy, strong pacing, annotated visuals, restrained interaction and careful transitions.
- The tone should be serious, clear and investigative, with moments of visual surprise.
